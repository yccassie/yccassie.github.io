An Accessible Breakaway Hanger for Everyday Dressing

Background

Getting dressed was already meaningful to the client: he enjoyed choosing outfits for different activities and used clothing as a form of communication and self-expression. The problem was not choosing what to wear — it was retrieving clothing from conventional hangers efficiently and independently.

From a seated position, the interaction with a standard hanger demanded more coordination than was practical. He tended to pull directly on the clothing rather than on the hanger. That could derail the hanger from the rod or break it, turning a simple dressing task into something that required repeated assistance.

The design challenge

How might we help the client retrieve clothing independently from a seated position — without breaking hangers, dropping them to the floor, or sacrificing closet capacity?

Understanding the user before designing the device

We began with a Zoom conversation with the client and a family member, then paired that input with contextual observation of his closet setup and dressing routine. Rather than treating the hanger as an isolated object, we looked at the full interaction: the clothing rod height, limited closet space, the client’s pulling motion, what happens when a hanger falls, and how often clothing choices happen throughout the day.

A few insights quickly became design-critical:

  • The device needed to work from a wheelchair-seated position and support one-handed interaction.
  • It had to tolerate pulling on the shirt itself, not assume the user would grasp the hanger hook.
  • A hanger falling to the floor was a major failure mode because the client could not easily retrieve it.
  • Closet capacity mattered, so the solution had to remain compact rather than add a bulky mechanism.
  • Shirts and heavier garments were high-priority items, so the connection had to be strong enough to hold weight but easy enough to release safely.

From observations to design requirements

The research shifted the problem from “make a better hanger” to a much more specific interaction goal: create a hanger that can stay securely on the rod while the clothing releases with low resistance. That reframing gave us a concrete set of requirements.

RequirementWhy it mattered
Break away under tensionPrevent hanger snapping or dangerous recoil when the shirt is pulled.
Stay attached to the closet rodAvoid derailment and eliminate the need to retrieve dropped hangers.
One-handed operationMatch the actual way the client interacts with clothing.
Compact formPreserve limited closet capacity.
Support heavier garmentsWork with sweatshirts, jerseys, coats, and everyday shirts.
Low-force releaseEnable independent use without requiring precise fine-motor control.

Market research

Before narrowing the mechanism, we reviewed existing hanger solutions to understand what the market already offered and where those products did not address the client’s specific interaction needs.

Exploring multiple mechanisms

Our early ideas included folding or collapsing hinges, magnetic detachment, alternative hook shapes, and different hanger materials. We also scanned existing products — collapsible hangers, hookless magnetic hangers, thicker plastic hangers, velvet hangers, and wooden hangers — to understand what already existed and where those products failed to address the client’s specific interaction.

What we ruled out — and why

We did not use a formal decision matrix. Instead, we combined verbal feedback, contextual observation, and repeated trial-and-error. The hinge concept was dropped because the geometry and pulling angle risked requiring more coordinated use of both upper extremities. A double-magnet approach was simplified to one central magnet. The open-hook idea also evolved into a closed connection so the hanger would not derail from the rod.

The concept: keep the rod connection, release the hanger body

The final concept separated the system into two functional layers. A large closed carabiner remained on the closet rod, while a magnetic breakaway connection joined that upper component to the hanger body. A swivel was added to absorb changes in pull angle and reduce stress on the connection. A wider 3D-printed bracket increased the support area at the magnet attachment point.

The key interaction was intentionally simple: pull the clothing as the client naturally would; the system should either let the garment slide off smoothly or allow the magnetic connection to release before the hanger breaks or flies off the rod.

Prototyping the physical system

The build used a standard plastic hanger as the base, magnets with screws, a swivel magnetic hook, 3D-printed carabiners and brackets, felt, and air-dry clay. Each material solved a specific constraint rather than serving as decoration.

  • The thicker plastic hanger provided a stronger base for modification.
  • The magnetic interface created an easy attach/detach mechanism.
  • The large 3D-printed carabiner formed a closed connection around the clothing rod.
  • The swivel reduced stress caused by off-angle pulling.
  • The bracket increased the magnet’s support area; air-dry clay reinforced the base.
  • Felt intentionally reduced magnetic strength so less pull force was needed for release.

The documented material cost was $46.99 for a 10-piece batch, with the hangers donated and the 3D-printed parts treated as no-cost in the project accounting. Once the materials were prepared, assembly took about 15 minutes per hanger, excluding print time for the custom components.

Testing what mattered: release, stability, and real clothing weight

Team testing focused on whether the prototype could hold real garments while still releasing safely under tension. The device was tested with heavier items including a winter coat and hockey jerseys. In those trials, the magnetic connection remained stable during hanging, the closed carabiner stayed on the rod, and the system allowed a low-resistance release without snapping the hanger.

Importantly, we separated internal prototype testing from client validation. At the time of the final presentation, the team had delivered a prototype and requested videos and feedback, but direct client feedback had not yet been received. That means the prototype showed strong functional promise, but it was not yet fully validated in the client’s everyday routine.

Outcome

The result was a functional breakaway hanger prototype designed around the client’s actual behavior rather than asking him to adapt to a conventional hanger. The system preserved the familiar hanger form, stayed compact in the closet, supported heavier clothing, and introduced a controlled release point before the hanger itself became the failure point.

The biggest shift was not making the hanger stronger. It was redesigning where the system should give way.

What worked — and what still needed refinement

What worked
  • Closed rod connection reduced derailment risk.
  • Magnetic release introduced a safer failure point.
  • Swivel helped accommodate off-angle pulling.
  • Prototype held heavier garments during team testing.
  • 3D printing made component dimensions customizable.
What needed more iteration
  • Fine-tune magnet strength and release force.
  • Experiment with magnet angle and centering.
  • Refine aesthetic finish and consistency.
  • Validate repeated everyday use with the client.
  • Quantify performance rather than rely only on observational testing.

Next steps

If I continued the project, I would focus on validation and tuning rather than adding more features:

  • Observe the client using the prototype across multiple dressing sessions and garment types.
  • Measure the pull force required for release and compare magnet strengths systematically.
  • Iterate the magnet angle and centering to improve consistency and appearance.
  • Evaluate long-term durability of the 3D-printed components, clay reinforcement, and felt interface.
  • Explore adjacent closet-access needs, including a visual outfit-selection system on the client’s AAC.
